# Crackdown (2007) — Research

> **Why this game is in our reference set:** It is the only game here that treats **urban verticality as the core mechanic rather than a feature**. Prototype gives us traversal; Crackdown gives us the reason the city is vertical and the loop that makes players look *up*. Its agility orb economy is the best existing model for "collectibles that teach a movement system."

---

## 1. Snapshot

| | |
|---|---|
| **Released** | February 2007 (Xbox 360) |
| **Developer** | Realtime Worlds (Dundee, Scotland) |
| **Publisher** | Microsoft Game Studios |
| **Creative Director** | **David Jones** — creator of *Lemmings* and *Grand Theft Auto* |
| **Setting** | Pacific City — a fictional, heavily vertical open world |
| **Sales** | 1.5 million by end of 2007; topped February 2007 charts |
| **Awards** | BAFTA 2007 — Best Action/Adventure **and** Best Use of Audio |
| **Development** | Began 2002 on original Xbox; moved to Xbox 360 in 2004 |
| **Engine** | Switched to **RenderWare 4** in 2005, causing significant problems; Microsoft supplied extra programmers to fix it |
| **Fate** | Realtime Worlds collapsed in 2010 after *APB: All Points Bulletin* failed |

**The Halo 3 beta story.** Crackdown **initially tested poorly**. Microsoft bundled Halo 3 beta access with it, which "proved crucial" in elevating player interest. Many players bought it for the beta and stayed for the orbs — an accidental but instructive case of a game whose quality was only apparent once you were actually playing it. Jones himself worried: *"This game does not look good in screenshots."*

> **A useful warning for us.** A game about movement feel is nearly impossible to sell in a still image. If our pitch's appeal is *how traversal feels*, our marketing burden is on motion — clips, GIFs, and moment-to-moment video. This is also, notably, how Megabonk succeeded.

## 2. Core Mechanics

### 2.1 The five skills and use-based progression

The player is a genetically enhanced Agent with **five independently levelling skills**:

| Skill | Governs | Levelled by |
|---|---|---|
| **Agility** | Jump height, running speed, swimming | Collecting **Agility Orbs**; rooftop traversal |
| **Strength** | Melee damage, lifting/throwing, max health | Melee kills, throwing objects |
| **Firearms** | Weapon accuracy, aim assist, damage | Gun kills |
| **Explosives** | Blast radius and damage | Grenade/rocket kills |
| **Driving** | Vehicle handling, and the Agency vehicles' transformations | Vehicular kills, driving |

**The defining property: you level a skill by using it.** Experience orbs drop from defeated enemies, and **the orb type depends on how you killed them**. Shoot an enemy, get Firearms orbs. Punch them, get Strength orbs. This means the player's build emerges from their play style automatically — no menu, no allocation, no decision. The game reads what you enjoy and amplifies it.

> This is meaningfully different from a survivors-like's offer-based progression, and it is worth considering as a *parallel* system rather than a replacement — see section 10.

### 2.2 Agility Orbs — the standout mechanic

**500 Agility Orbs** are scattered across Pacific City, almost all of them placed **on rooftops, ledges, antennae, and high structures**. There are an additional **300 Hidden Orbs**, for **800 total**. Collecting all of them awards the "Free Runner" achievement.

The orbs have three properties that make them work:

1. **They glow bright green and emit an audible hum.** They are perceptible before they are visible. Players hear an orb they cannot see, and go looking. The audio cue is doing an enormous amount of work — and Crackdown won a BAFTA for audio.
2. **They are placed at the top of things.** Agility orbs train the player to look up, then to climb, then to plan routes. The collectible *is* the tutorial for the traversal system.
3. **They directly upgrade the ability used to reach them.** Collecting agility orbs raises Agility, which raises jump height, which puts previously unreachable orbs in range. **The reward for climbing is a better climb.**

That third point is the design's real elegance: it is a **self-reinforcing loop with a built-in difficulty curve**. Low-agility players collect low orbs; each orb raises the ceiling; the city progressively opens vertically. No gating, no keys, no explicit progression walls — the city's accessibility is a continuous function of a stat the player raises by playing.

**Why it is compulsive.** Jones described it directly: agility orbs "created a collection mechanism that drew people up," and "people loved the verticality, being able to go up into the city, and the platforming aspects." The community's own language is the tell — guides joke about collection "addiction," and "one more orb" is the canonical description of a Crackdown session ending at 3am.

### 2.3 The sandbox

- Three crime syndicates, each with a hierarchy of named bosses
- **Nonlinear**: any boss can be attacked at any time, in any order, by any method
- Killing an underboss weakens the organisation in ways that make the kingpin easier — a soft dependency graph rather than a hard sequence
- Wide latitude in approach: snipe from a rooftop, drive a truck into a compound, throw a car, take a helicopter

### 2.4 Physics and the city as a toy

Strength scaling turns the environment into ammunition — cars, dumpsters, and eventually buses become throwables. The world's props scale in usefulness with the player's stats, which means the same street is a different toolbox at Strength 1 and Strength 5.

## 3. Design Philosophy (derived)

1. **Make the collectible teach the mechanic.** Agility orbs are not padding; they are a distributed, self-paced traversal tutorial disguised as loot.
2. **Reward the verb with more of the verb.** Climbing gives you climbing power. The loop closes on itself.
3. **Progression should read your play, not ask you.** Use-based levelling means no allocation screens and no wrong builds.
4. **Place rewards where you want eyes.** The city is vertical because the orbs are up there. Player attention follows loot placement absolutely.
5. **Audio is a navigation system.** An orb you can hear but not see is a quest. This is nearly free to implement and extremely effective in a vertical space where line of sight is constantly broken.
6. **Let the world scale with the player.** Props that become weapons as Strength rises means content re-reads itself at every power tier.
7. **Order is the player's business.** Nonlinear targets, any approach.

---

## 4. Why It Is Fun (what players actually say)

- **"One more orb."** The most consistent report is the compulsion loop of orb collection. Players describe sessions vanishing into rooftop hopping with no mission in sight.
- **Jumping like a mutant kangaroo.** A Realtime Worlds developer's own description of what players enjoy — "jumping like a mutant kangaroo from rooftop to rooftop" — and the community adopted it.
- **Verticality as pleasure in itself.** Jones's own read: people loved going *up* into the city. The platforming was the draw, not the crime-fighting.
- **Visible, continuous growth.** Because Agility raises jump height in visible increments, the player feels progress in the most tangible possible way: the building you could not clear last hour, you clear now.
- **Emergent sandbox play.** Praised for emergent gameplay and replayability — the combination of physics, superpowers, and a nonlinear target list produced stories players made themselves.

---

## 5. What Makes It Stand Out

**The upgrade loop is the traversal loop.** No other game in our reference set binds the collectible, the tutorial, the progression, and the world's accessibility into one mechanic this tightly. This is the single most transferable idea in this document.

**A city built to be climbed.** Pacific City's architecture was designed *around* orb placement and jump arcs, not as a realistic city that happens to be climbable. The level design serves the movement system.

**Audio-led discovery.** The orb hum is one of the most effective and cheapest navigation affordances in open-world design.

**Genuine co-op sandbox.** Two-player drop-in co-op across the whole city, at a time when that was rare.

## 6. Concrete Tuning Numbers

| Dial | Value |
|---|---|
| **Agility Orbs** | **500** |
| **Hidden Orbs** | **300** |
| **Total orbs** | **800** |
| **Skills** | 5 (Agility, Strength, Firearms, Explosives, Driving) |
| **Skill levels** | 5 star-ranked tiers per skill (plus a maxed state) |
| **Crime syndicates** | 3 |
| **Sales** | 1.5M in year one |

**The orb curve (qualitative).** Early agility orbs sit on low walls, fire escapes, and one-storey rooftops. Mid-tier orbs sit on mid-rise buildings and require a two-jump chain. Late orbs sit on the tallest towers in the city and require maxed agility plus route planning. **The placement distribution itself encodes the difficulty curve** — no explicit gating needed.

**What is notable about 500.** It is a very large number for a collectible, and it works because each one is a small, immediate hit of both reward and progress. The genre-relevant lesson is about *density of reward events*, which is exactly the Vampire Survivors lesson arrived at from a completely different direction: **something good every few seconds**.

## 7. Failure Modes and Criticism

**Weak storytelling.** The most consistent critical note. The narrative is thin and the twist is under-supported. Nobody played Crackdown for the story, and the game barely tried.

> For us this is close to a non-issue — run-based games carry almost no narrative load — but it is a reminder that a strong central mechanic can absorb a weak everything-else, exactly as it did for Prototype.

**"Does not look good in screenshots."** Jones's own concern, and it nearly sank the game. It tested poorly and needed the Halo 3 beta to find an audience. The mechanic only reads in motion.

**Repetitive mission structure.** The core content is "go to marker, kill boss," repeated 21 times. The sandbox is the appeal; the objectives are a thin scaffold.

**Combat is the weakest system.** Shooting is serviceable, aim-assist-heavy, and clearly secondary to traversal. Once maxed, encounters lose tension.

**Ceiling problem.** Once Agility is maxed and all orbs are collected, the city's vertical challenge is gone — the player can reach everything, and the thing that made the city interesting is exhausted. **This is the same post-completion collapse Vampire Survivors has**, arriving from a different mechanism.

**Engine trouble.** The 2005 switch to RenderWare 4 caused significant problems and required Microsoft to supply additional programmers. A cautionary note about mid-production technology changes.

**Studio outcome.** Realtime Worlds collapsed in 2010 after APB. Crackdown's success did not protect it.

### The lessons, stated plainly

1. **A movement-driven game cannot be sold in a still frame.** Plan marketing around motion from the start.
2. **Maxed traversal exhausts a fixed city.** If our traversal ceiling is reachable, our city must keep escalating — procedural layouts, or per-run verticality requirements, or a moving goalpost.
3. **A great core loop tolerates weak objectives, but only for one game.** Crackdown 2 reused the formula and was received far worse.
4. **Do not switch engines mid-production.** Decide early, prototype the hard case first.

## 8. Meta-Progression and Retention

Crackdown is a **campaign**, not a run-based game, so its structure does not map directly. But the shape is instructive:

| Layer | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| **Primary progression** | Five use-based skills, levelled implicitly by play style |
| **Collection drip** | 800 orbs distributed across the city at escalating difficulty |
| **Objective structure** | 3 syndicates × hierarchy of bosses, nonlinear |
| **Completion goal** | "Free Runner" achievement for all orbs |
| **Co-op** | Two-player drop-in across the whole world |

**What transfers to a run-based game:**

- **Orbs as an in-run collectible tied to traversal.** A survivors-like already has XP gems on the ground. Crackdown's insight is that **placing some of them high** converts a pickup into a traversal decision. That is nearly free for us and is arguably the cleanest single mechanic for uniting our two genres.
- **Use-based implicit progression** as a *secondary* system alongside offer-based upgrades. Reward what the player actually does.
- **Escalating placement as a difficulty curve** — early-run pickups at street level, late-run pickups at height.

**What does not transfer:** the fixed 800-orb completion list. In a run-based game the equivalent must regenerate per run, or become a meta-layer.

## 9. Technical Profile

**Engine.** Development began in 2002 targeting the original Xbox, moved to Xbox 360 in 2004, and **switched to RenderWare 4 in 2005**. That switch "caused significant problems" and Microsoft had to supply additional programmers to resolve them. This is the clearest documented technical fact about the project and it is a cautionary one.

**Platform.** Xbox 360 exclusive — a single fixed hardware target, which is a significant advantage when building a dense, fully-climbable city.

**What is not publicly documented.** Realtime Worlds published no detailed technical postmortem covering streaming, LOD, collision authoring for climbable geometry, or NPC density systems. Specific figures on entity counts or draw distances are not available.

**What the design implies technically.** A fully climbable city means **every surface needs meaningful collision authored at player scale**, not just the walkable ground plane. That is a substantial content cost and a substantial runtime cost, and it is the technical price of the verticality we want. Crackdown's cel-shaded, low-detail art direction is plausibly in part an answer to that cost — simpler geometry, readable silhouettes, cheaper collision. Our own art direction should be chosen with this in mind rather than fighting it.

**Relevance to our horde problem:** none directly. Crackdown has no hordes. Its contribution is city architecture and traversal economy, not crowd technology.

## 10. Carry Forward / Leave Behind

### ✅ Carry forward

| What | Why |
|---|---|
| **Collectibles placed at height** | The single best idea here. Putting XP/pickups on rooftops converts our traversal from an escape tool into a *reason to climb into danger*. Possibly the key to our central design tension. |
| **The reward for climbing is a better climb** | Self-reinforcing traversal loop with a built-in difficulty curve. Agility-style upgrades that open new routes mid-run. |
| **Audible pickup cues** | An orb you can hear but not see is a goal. Cheap, and essential in a vertical space with constantly broken line of sight. |
| **Escalating vertical placement as a difficulty curve** | Street level early, rooftops late. No explicit gating needed. |
| **Use-based implicit progression** | Reward the verbs the player actually chooses. Good as a secondary layer under offer-based upgrades. |
| **A city designed around jump arcs** | Architecture in service of the movement system, not realism. |
| **World props scaling with player power** | The same street re-reads as a different toolbox at each power tier. |
| **Readable, simplified art direction** | Serves navigation, collision cost, and silhouette legibility in a vertical space. |

### ❌ Leave behind

| What | Why |
|---|---|
| **Fixed finite collectible list** | Needs to regenerate per run or become a meta-layer. |
| **Exhaustible vertical challenge** | Once you can reach everything, the city stops being interesting. Our verticality must keep escalating. |
| **"Go to marker, kill boss" objective structure** | We have no missions. |
| **Combat as an afterthought** | Combat is our entire second half. Crackdown's shooting is not a model. |
| **Mid-production engine switching** | Decide early. Prototype the horde case before committing. |

### 🔑 The idea to take most seriously

**Crackdown is the best available answer to the Prototype/Vampire Survivors conflict.**

The conflict: our traversal lets the player escape a horde that needs to surround them.

Crackdown's mechanism, applied: **put the things the player needs at height.** If XP, chests, upgrade pickups, and objectives are on rooftops and ledges, then climbing is not escape — climbing is *where the run is won*. The player goes up because they must, and the design problem becomes "how do enemies threaten a player at altitude," which is a far more tractable question than "how do we stop the player from leaving."

This is tracked as the leading candidate resolution in `docs/research/00-synthesis.md`.
