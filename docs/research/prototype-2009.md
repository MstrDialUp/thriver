# Prototype (2009) — Research

> **Why this game is in our reference set:** It is the urban power fantasy. Prototype is where our city, our traversal, our overpowered weapons, and our chaos generation come from. It is *not* where our run structure or progression math comes from.

---

## 1. Snapshot

| | |
|---|---|
| **Released** | June 9, 2009 (PS3, Xbox 360, Windows) |
| **Developer** | Radical Entertainment (Vancouver) |
| **Publisher** | Activision (post-Vivendi merger, 2008) |
| **Genre** | Open-world action-adventure |
| **Setting** | Manhattan, New York City, under viral outbreak |
| **Metacritic** | 78–79 / 100 |
| **Sales** | ~2.1 million copies by March 2012; topped June 2009 charts |
| **Direct ancestor** | *The Incredible Hulk: Ultimate Destruction* (Radical, 2005) |
| **Fate** | Sequel in 2012 underperformed; Activision downsized Radical the same year |

Prototype launched into a head-to-head rivalry with Sucker Punch's *inFamous*, released three weeks earlier. The two games split the "superpowered open-world city" space between them, and the comparison shaped how both were received.

## 2. Core Mechanics

### 2.1 Traversal — the actual product

Alex Mercer's movement is the game's real content. The design target was **constant momentum with no interruption**:

- **Sprint auto-vaults.** Holding sprint makes Alex automatically clear obstacles — cars, railings, fences, low walls. The player never stops to negotiate geometry.
- **Wall-running.** Run at a building and Alex runs *up* it, vertically, for a sustained distance before needing a jump to continue.
- **Superjump.** Clears most buildings. Charged for extra height.
- **Glide.** Held after a jump, converting altitude into distance.
- **No fall damage.** Ever. Height is pure upside with zero risk.
- **Air dash.** Extends glide chains and corrects mid-air trajectory.

Radical described the movement model as parkour — move from point A to point B as fast as possible, with the environment as an accelerant rather than an obstacle. The critical property: **these systems chain**. Wall-run into superjump into glide into air-dash into wall-run is a single continuous input flow, and skilled players cross Manhattan without ever touching the ground.

### 2.2 Consume — resource, healing, narrative, and stealth in one verb

Alex absorbs a target's biomass, which simultaneously:

- Restores health
- Feeds the biomass pool used for Critical Mass
- Absorbs the victim's **memories and skills** (the story delivery mechanism — the "Web of Intrigue")
- Grants Alex the victim's **appearance as a disguise**

One button does four jobs. This is unusually efficient design and worth studying on its own merits.

### 2.3 Disguise and the stealth layer

Consuming a soldier lets Alex walk into a military base as that soldier. The disguise holds only while inconspicuous — **using any offensive or defensive power immediately breaks it**. This creates a deliberate tension valve: the player chooses between the loud fantasy and the quiet infiltration, and the game punishes neither.

### 2.4 Shapeshifting weapons

Alex transforms body parts into weapons. Only one is equipped at a time; swapping is instant via a radial menu.

| Weapon | Role |
|---|---|
| **Claws** | Fast, wide crowd clear; ground-spike special hits an area |
| **Blade** | High single-target damage; the "boss killer" |
| **Hammerfists** | Slow, heavy, armor-breaking; anti-vehicle and anti-armor |
| **Whipfist** | Long range, grapple, anti-air; pulls targets in |
| **Musclemass** | Passive strength buff for throwing objects |

Plus defensive forms — **Shield** (directional block) and **Armor** (flat damage reduction at the cost of mobility).

### 2.5 Critical Mass and Devastators

Accumulating enough biomass puts Alex into **Critical Mass**, which unlocks **Devastators** — screen-clearing ultimates:

- **Tendril Barrage** — impaling tendrils fire outward in all directions
- **Groundspike Graveyard** — massive spikes erupt from the ground in a radius
- **Critical Pain** — a single concentrated beam, single-target burst

This is the game's explicit "earned chaos" release valve: consume enough, then detonate.

### 2.6 Hijacking and weapons of war

Alex commandeers tanks and helicopters by leaping onto them and tearing out the crew. He also rips turrets off mounts and carries them. The city's military hardware is a pickup, not an obstacle.

### 2.7 The three-faction city

Manhattan is divided into contested territory:

- **Blue Zones** — military-controlled
- **Red Zones** — infected-dominated
- **Purple Zones** — active war between the two

Military and Infected fight *each other* independently of the player. Player actions shift zone control over the campaign. This is the chaos-generation engine: the city is already violent before you arrive, and you are a third participant escalating an existing conflict.

## 3. Design Philosophy (derived)

These are the principles the mechanics imply. Stated as rules, because these are the ones we may want to adopt.

1. **Movement is the reward, not the tax.** Most open-world games treat travel as dead time and solve it with fast travel. Prototype makes travel the most enjoyable thing in the game. Fast travel would have been a downgrade.
2. **Never interrupt momentum.** Auto-vault, no fall damage, no stamina meter, no climbing minigame. Every system that could stop the player was deleted.
3. **Verticality is free.** Height costs nothing and is never punished. The player is trained within minutes that up is always safe, which makes them use the Z axis constantly.
4. **The city is ammunition.** Cars, people, tanks, helicopters, lampposts — all pickups. Nothing in the environment is inert.
5. **The world fights without you.** Faction warfare runs autonomously, so the world reads as a living disaster rather than a set of dormant encounters waiting for a trigger.
6. **One verb, many payloads.** Consume handles healing, resources, stealth, and story. Fewer verbs, each doing more.
7. **Power is granted, not negotiated.** The game opens with Alex already near-godlike and spends the campaign widening the gap. There is no humbling phase.

## 4. Why It Is Fun (what players actually say)

The consensus, across reviews and retrospectives, lands on two things — and notably **not** on the story or mission design:

- **Traversal is the addiction.** Players describe simply moving through Manhattan as the reason to keep playing. Free-running the skyline is self-justifying; people report loading the game to move around without doing missions.
- **Chaotic engagement is what hooks.** The pleasure is in the pandemonium — crowds, military, infected, explosions, and the player at the center of it with a dozen tools. The game gives many options at once and the fun is in choosing among them fast.
- **Unrestrained power.** Radical wanted players to feel like a destructive force. The game does not gate this behind a slow ramp; it hands it over and escalates.
- **Combat and traversal are one system.** You do not stop moving to fight. You glide into a crowd, tendril-whip a helicopter out of the sky, land on a tank, throw it, and keep running. The seam between the two modes is deliberately invisible.

**The takeaway for us:** the moment-to-moment feel carried a game whose story and missions were widely called mediocre. That is a strong signal about where budget belongs.

## 5. What Makes It Stand Out

**Ultimate Destruction's DNA in a real city.** Radical had already solved "superhero traversal in an urban grid" with the 2005 Hulk game. Prototype is that system refined and given a darker skin. Few studios got a second pass at their own best mechanic.

**Shapeshifting as a weapon wheel.** Other power-fantasy games give you guns. Prototype's arsenal *is* your body, and swapping is instantaneous with no reload, no ammo, no pickup. The weapon system has zero friction.

**Consume as a multi-tool.** Healing, resource, stealth, and story delivery in one input is a genuinely elegant piece of design that few games have copied.

**Crowd density as a feature.** The streets are full of civilians and soldiers, and all of them are usable — as food, as disguise, as projectiles. Crowds are content, not set dressing.

## 6. Concrete Tuning Numbers

> **Honest caveat:** Prototype predates the era of comprehensive public wikis with datamined values, and Radical published no technical postmortem. Unlike the survivors-likes in this research set, precise internal numbers are largely unavailable. What follows is what is verifiable; where a number is not public, this document says so rather than inventing one.

**What is established:**

- Sales: ~2.1M by March 2012
- Metacritic: 78–79
- Campaign length: broadly reported in the 12–15 hour range for the main story, considerably longer for full collectible completion
- Collectibles: hundreds of **Landmarks**, **Hint markers**, and **Web of Intrigue** targets scattered across Manhattan, plus scattered movement-challenge events

**Progression currency:** **Evolution Points (EP)**, earned from kills, consumes, missions, collectibles, and challenge events, spent in a menu on unlocking and upgrading powers, movement abilities, and health. This is an unusual structure for our purposes — it is a *single-run-length campaign* economy, not a per-run economy, and does not map directly onto a bullet heaven.

**What is not publicly documented and should not be assumed:**

- Exact biomass thresholds for Critical Mass
- Per-power EP costs
- Enemy health/damage tables
- Spawn density caps or crowd entity budgets

**Design conclusion for us:** Prototype gives us *feel* targets, not *math* targets. Our numbers must come from the survivors-likes. Our motion curves and power-grant pacing can come from here.

## 7. Failure Modes and Criticism

This is the section that protects us from inheriting flaws along with strengths.

**Repetitive mission design.** The most consistent criticism. Missions reduce to a small set of templates — kill this, escort this, destroy that, reach here within a time limit — reused across the campaign. The traversal stayed fun; the tasks did not.

**Bland visuals and art direction.** Manhattan is grey, brown, and grey. Reviewers repeatedly called the art style drab. A city built for verticality that is visually monotonous makes navigation harder *and* makes the spectacle land softer.

**Camera and control complaints.** Reception noted criticism of the control scheme. The practical failure is well known to players: in dense combat, with a fast-moving player and a fast camera, target acquisition becomes mush. Lock-on struggles to pick the intended target from a crowd. **This is a direct warning for us** — we are planning far higher entity counts than Prototype ever had, in 3D, with verticality. Prototype's camera already strained at its density; ours will be tested far harder.

**Difficulty spikes.** Certain encounters — notably armored/boss-tier enemies — abruptly break the power fantasy by demanding precision the rest of the game never asked for and the camera does not support.

**Shallowness under repetition.** The critique that sank the sequel — "too shallow, and as such, too repetitive" — is latent in the first game too. The combat's depth is in *option variety*, not in mastery depth. Once a player finds an effective tool, there is little pressure to use the rest.

**The series' commercial end.** Prototype 2 failed to sustain buzz, Activision judged its investment unjustified, and Radical was gutted in 2012. The novelty of the traversal fantasy was the asset; when the sequel offered the same novelty a second time, it was no longer novel.

### The lessons, stated plainly

1. A great movement system does not carry weak content forever. It carried Prototype 1 and failed to carry Prototype 2.
2. Camera and target legibility in dense combat is an unsolved problem in this game. **We must solve it; we cannot inherit it.**
3. Option variety is not the same as depth. If one tool dominates, the other eleven are decoration. (The survivors-likes solve this with synergy requirements — see the Vampire Survivors and Megabonk docs.)
4. Visual monotony undercuts both navigation and spectacle in a vertical city.

## 8. Meta-Progression and Retention

**There essentially is none, in our sense.** Prototype is a linear campaign with an in-campaign upgrade economy (EP). There is no run loop, no between-run currency, no persistent unlock drip across sessions. Retention came from campaign length plus collectible hunting plus a New Game+ that carried powers forward.

**What this means for us:** Prototype contributes *nothing* to our meta-progression design. That entire system must come from Vampire Survivors, Megabonk, and Crackdown's orb economy. Prototype's contribution is the in-run power curve shape — start strong, end absurd — and the feel of each individual grant.

**One transferable idea:** the EP → menu-unlock structure resembles a survivors-like's between-run shop. But Prototype spends it over ~14 hours; we would spend it over hundreds of 20-minute runs. The pacing does not transfer; only the shape does.

## 9. Technical Profile

**Engine.** Radical's proprietary engine. *Prototype 2* is documented as running Radical's **"Titanium 2.0"** engine with DirectX 10 rendering; the 2009 original ran an earlier iteration of the same in-house technology. Radical did not publish detailed architecture documentation, and no technical postmortem is publicly available.

**Platforms.** PS3, Xbox 360, Windows — a 2009 console generation target. This is important context for the entity counts below: Prototype's density was achieved on ~512MB of unified memory and hardware roughly three orders of magnitude weaker than a modern target.

**Crowd systems.** Prototype's streets carried dense civilian and military crowds that the player could interact with individually — consume, throw, disguise as. Crowd density was one of the primary performance dials exposed to PC players; community optimization guidance points at **lowering crowd density and shadows ahead of texture quality** for framerate recovery, which tells us crowd simulation was a dominant cost in the frame budget.

**The critical distinction for our project:** Prototype's crowds were *ambient NPCs* — mostly passive, cheap, low-AI, not all simultaneously hostile and pathing toward the player. A bullet heaven's hordes are *combat entities*: each one pathfinds toward the player, resolves collisions against every neighbor, takes damage from many sources per frame, and dies with feedback. These are not comparable workloads.

**Conclusion:** Prototype does not provide us with a horde architecture. It provides evidence that a dense, fully interactive city is achievable, and a warning that crowd cost dominates the frame budget. Our horde technology must come from elsewhere — see the technical sections of the Vampire Survivors, Megabonk, and Risk of Rain 2 documents, and the synthesis doc's open questions.

## 10. Carry Forward / Leave Behind

### ✅ Carry forward

| What | Why |
|---|---|
| **Chained, uninterrupted traversal** | Wall-run → jump → glide → dash as one flow. This is our movement thesis. Verticality must feel this good or the urban setting is wasted. |
| **No fall damage, no stamina** | Delete every system that punishes or interrupts vertical movement. Height must be free. |
| **Auto-vault on sprint** | Never make the player negotiate small geometry. Especially critical when a horde is chasing. |
| **The city as ammunition** | Cars, debris, signage, vehicles as throwables and weapons. Turns environment into build variety. |
| **Instant, frictionless weapon swapping** | No reload, no ammo, no pickup delay. Fits a bullet heaven perfectly. |
| **Earned screen-clearing ultimates** | The Devastator/Critical Mass loop is exactly the "release valve" a bullet heaven's pressure curve wants. |
| **Autonomous faction conflict** | Enemies fighting each other makes the city read as a living disaster. Also a potential horde-thinning mechanic — see synthesis. |
| **Consume as a multi-purpose verb** | One input for heal + resource + something else is very strong economy design. |
| **Power granted early and escalated** | No humbling phase. Matches the bullet heaven curve shape. |

### ❌ Leave behind

| What | Why |
|---|---|
| **Mission-template campaign structure** | We are run-based. Prototype's repetition problem is its mission templates; we simply do not have missions. |
| **The camera and lock-on model** | Known weak point at *Prototype's* density. Fatal at ours. This needs original work, not inheritance. |
| **Drab monochrome city art** | Vertical navigation demands landmark legibility and color-coded readability. Visual monotony is a mechanical problem, not just an aesthetic one. |
| **Ambient-crowd technical approach** | Wrong workload class for combat hordes. |
| **EP-style long-form campaign economy** | Wrong time scale for run-based play. |
| **Option-variety-as-depth** | Without synergy pressure, players settle on one tool. Our build system must actively reward breadth. |

### ⚠️ The unresolved tension

**Prototype's traversal exists so you can escape. A bullet heaven requires you to be surrounded.**

Every movement ability listed in the "carry forward" column is, from a horde-pressure standpoint, an *escape tool*. A player who can wall-run up a skyscraper and glide away has trivially defeated a ground-bound horde. This is the central design problem our project inherits from combining these references, and it is tracked as the primary open question in `docs/research/00-synthesis.md` and `docs/design/design-doc.md`.
